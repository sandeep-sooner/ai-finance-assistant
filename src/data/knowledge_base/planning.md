# Setting Financial Goals
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/define-your-goals

A financial goal turns a wish into a plan. A useful goal states what the money is for, how much is needed and by when. "Save 30,000 dollars for a house deposit in five years" can be planned; "save more" cannot.

Goals are usually grouped by timeline. Short-term goals are under about three years, such as an emergency fund or a holiday. Medium-term goals are three to ten years, such as a house deposit. Long-term goals are beyond ten years, such as retirement.

The timeline guides where the money goes: short-term money belongs in stable places, and long-term money can be invested for growth. Once a goal has an amount and a date you can work out the monthly saving required and check progress each year.

# How Much to Save Each Month
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/f/futurevalue.asp

The monthly amount a goal needs depends on four things: the target, the time available, what you have already saved, and the return you assume.

With no growth the sum is simple: 24,000 dollars in four years is 500 dollars a month. Investment growth lowers the amount. To reach 100,000 dollars in 20 years from nothing you would need about 417 dollars a month with no return, but about 216 dollars a month at an assumed 6 percent yearly return, because growth supplies the rest.

Time is the strongest lever. Waiting ten years to start roughly doubles or triples the monthly amount needed for the same retirement target. If a plan falls short there are only a few levers: save more, allow more time, lower the target, or accept more risk in search of a higher return, which also raises the chance of falling short.

# Retirement Saving Basics
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/retirement-toolkit

Retirement is usually the largest and longest goal. Because it may need to fund 25 or 30 years of living costs, it relies heavily on decades of compounding.

Common rules of thumb include saving around 15 percent of pre-tax income including any employer contribution, and aiming for savings milestones such as one year's salary by age 30 and about ten times salary by retirement age. These are rough guides, not rules.

Typical steps are: contribute enough to a workplace plan to get the full employer match, use tax-advantaged accounts such as a 401(k) or IRA, invest for growth while retirement is far away, and move gradually toward stability as it approaches. Starting early matters most: money invested at 25 has about 40 years to grow, twice as long as money invested at 45.

# The 4 Percent Rule
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/f/four-percent-rule.asp

The 4 percent rule is a guideline for how much a retiree can withdraw each year without running out of money. It suggests withdrawing 4 percent of the portfolio in the first year of retirement and then raising that dollar amount with inflation each year. Based on historical US returns, a balanced portfolio lasted at least 30 years in most past periods using this approach.

Turned around, it gives a rough savings target: about 25 times your yearly spending from the portfolio. Someone who needs 40,000 dollars a year would aim for about 1,000,000 dollars.

It is a starting point, not a guarantee. It is based on past US markets, a 30-year retirement and a fixed pattern of spending. Longer retirements, lower future returns or a poor first few years can make a lower rate safer.

# Paying Off Debt or Investing
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/articles/pf/08/invest-reduce-debt.asp

Whether to pay down debt or invest comes down to comparing the interest rate on the debt with the return you might reasonably expect from investing, remembering that debt repayment is a certain, risk-free return and investment returns are not.

High-interest debt, such as credit cards charging 20 percent, costs more than investments can be expected to earn, so paying it off first is usually the stronger choice. Low-interest debt, such as a mortgage at 4 percent, is closer to a judgement call.

A common order is: make all minimum payments, build a small emergency fund, capture any employer retirement match because it is an immediate return, pay off high-interest debt, then invest more. Peace of mind counts too; some people prefer being debt-free even when the arithmetic is close.

# Budgeting With the 50/30/20 Rule
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/022916/what-502030-budget-rule.asp

The 50/30/20 rule is a simple way to divide after-tax income. About 50 percent goes to needs such as housing, food, utilities, transport and minimum debt payments. About 30 percent goes to wants such as dining out, travel and entertainment. About 20 percent goes to savings, investing and extra debt repayment.

Example: on 4,000 dollars a month after tax, that is 2,000 for needs, 1,200 for wants and 800 for saving and investing.

The percentages are a starting point. In expensive cities needs may take more than half, and people with ambitious goals may save more than 20 percent. The lasting idea is to decide the saving amount first and automate it, often called paying yourself first, rather than saving whatever happens to be left.

# Choosing a Risk Profile
source: FINRA
url: https://www.finra.org/investors/investing/investing-basics/risk

A risk profile summarises how much investment risk suits a goal. Planning tools commonly use three.

A conservative profile holds mostly bonds and cash with a small share of stocks. It aims to protect the money, has smaller swings and lower expected growth, and suits short timelines or low tolerance for losses. A balanced profile holds a moderate mix, often around 60 percent stocks and 40 percent bonds, seeking growth with moderate swings. A growth profile holds mostly stocks. It has the highest expected long-term return and the largest drops, and suits long timelines and investors who can stay invested through downturns.

Higher assumed returns make a plan look easier but they are not guaranteed, and the range of possible results is wider. The profile should be led by the goal's timeline first and comfort with losses second.

# Saving for a Home Down Payment
source: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau
url: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/

A down payment is the part of a home's price you pay up front. Lenders in the United States often look for 20 percent to avoid private mortgage insurance, though many loans accept less. On a 300,000 dollar home, 20 percent is 60,000 dollars, plus closing costs that commonly run 2 to 5 percent of the price.

Because this is usually a short or medium-term goal with a fixed date, protecting the money matters more than growing it. Savers often use high-yield savings accounts, certificates of deposit or short-term bond funds. Money needed in under about three years is generally kept out of stocks, because a market fall could delay the purchase.

A plan is the target divided over the months available: 60,000 dollars in five years is 1,000 dollars a month before interest.

# Lump Sum or Regular Investing
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/l/lump-sum-payment.asp

When you receive a large amount, such as a bonus or inheritance, you can invest it all at once or spread it over several months. Historically, investing the lump sum immediately has produced a higher final value in roughly two out of three periods, simply because markets have risen more often than they have fallen and the money spends more time invested.

Spreading the investment out lowers the risk of investing everything just before a fall, and many people find it easier to stick with. The cost is that money waiting in cash usually earns less.

Either way, the more important decisions are the overall asset allocation and staying invested. For regular income such as a salary the question does not arise: investing each month as the money arrives is the natural approach.

# Education Savings and 529 Plans
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/529-plans

A 529 plan is a US tax-advantaged account for education costs. Contributions are made with after-tax money, grow free of federal tax, and can be withdrawn tax-free when used for qualified education expenses such as tuition, fees, books and housing. Many states also give a state tax deduction or credit for contributions.

Most plans offer age-based portfolios that begin mostly in stocks when the child is young and move toward bonds and cash as college approaches, similar to a target-date fund.

Withdrawals for other purposes are generally subject to income tax and a 10 percent penalty on the earnings. Rules and limits change, so check current official guidance. Starting early helps: 200 dollars a month for 18 years is 43,200 dollars of contributions, and at an assumed 5 percent return it could grow to about 70,000 dollars.

# Reviewing and Adjusting a Plan
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/five-questions-ask-you-invest

A financial plan is not set once and left. A yearly review, or one after a major life event such as a new job, marriage, a child or a home purchase, keeps it realistic.

A review checks a few things. Is the goal still the right amount and date? Are contributions on track, and can they rise with income? Has the portfolio drifted from its target mix and does it need rebalancing? Have costs or fees changed? Is the level of risk still right as the goal gets closer?

Reviewing too often can hurt: reacting to every market move tends to lead to buying high and selling low. A simple yearly routine of raising contributions and rebalancing is usually enough.
