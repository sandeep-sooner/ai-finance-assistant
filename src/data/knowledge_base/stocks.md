# What Is a Stock?
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/stocks

A stock, also called a share or equity, is a small piece of ownership in a company. If a company has one million shares and you own 1,000, you own 0.1 percent of it. Shareholders can make money in two ways: the share price can rise, and the company may pay part of its profit as dividends.

Companies sell stock to raise money to grow. After the first sale, shares trade between investors on exchanges such as the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq.

Stocks have offered higher long-term returns than bonds or cash, but with more risk. Prices can fall sharply, and if a company fails, shareholders are paid last and may lose everything. Holding many stocks, for example through an index fund, reduces the damage any one company can do.

# How Stock Prices Move
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/s/stock.asp

A stock's price is set by supply and demand. When more investors want to buy than sell, the price rises; when more want to sell, it falls. What changes that balance is new information about how much profit the company is expected to make in the future.

Common drivers include earnings reports, new products, changes in management, interest rates, the health of the economy and overall investor mood. Prices react to surprises: a company can report higher profit and still fall if investors expected even more.

In the short term prices are noisy and hard to predict. Over long periods they tend to follow the growth of company earnings. This is why daily moves say little about a long-term investment.

# Dividends
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/d/dividend.asp

A dividend is a payment a company makes to its shareholders, usually in cash and usually every quarter, out of its profits. Not all companies pay them. Mature, steady businesses often do, while fast-growing companies tend to reinvest profits instead.

Dividend yield is the yearly dividend divided by the share price. A stock priced at 100 dollars that pays 3 dollars a year yields 3 percent. A very high yield is not always good news: it can mean the price has fallen because investors expect the dividend to be cut.

Key dates: you must own the stock before the ex-dividend date to receive the next payment. Many investors reinvest dividends to buy more shares, which adds to compounding. Dividends are generally taxable in a regular brokerage account.

# Market Capitalization
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/m/marketcapitalization.asp

Market capitalization, or market cap, is the total market value of a company's shares: share price multiplied by the number of shares. A company with 50 million shares trading at 20 dollars has a market cap of 1 billion dollars.

Market cap, not share price, tells you how big a company is. A 500 dollar stock can be a smaller company than a 50 dollar stock.

Companies are often grouped by size. Large-cap companies are roughly 10 billion dollars and above and tend to be more established and stable. Mid-caps are roughly 2 to 10 billion. Small-caps are below about 2 billion and can grow faster but are usually more volatile. Most major indexes, including the S&P 500, weight companies by market cap, so the largest companies have the most influence.

# Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/price-earningsratio.asp

The price-to-earnings ratio compares a company's share price with its earnings per share. A stock at 60 dollars with earnings of 3 dollars per share has a P/E of 20, meaning investors pay 20 dollars for each dollar of yearly profit.

A high P/E usually means investors expect strong future growth; a low P/E can mean the stock is cheap or that the business has problems. The number only means something in comparison: against the company's own history, against similar companies in the same industry, or against the market as a whole.

Trailing P/E uses the last 12 months of earnings; forward P/E uses forecasts. P/E does not work for companies with no profit, and it says nothing about debt. It is a starting point for questions, not a buy or sell signal.

# Growth and Value Stocks
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/g/growthstock.asp

Growth stocks are companies expected to increase sales and profits faster than average. They often reinvest profits rather than pay dividends, and they tend to trade at high prices relative to current earnings. Their prices can fall hard when growth disappoints or interest rates rise.

Value stocks trade at low prices relative to measures such as earnings or book value, often because the business is mature, out of favour or facing difficulties. They more often pay dividends.

Neither style wins all the time: growth and value have each led for years at a stretch. Many investors hold both, for example through a broad market index fund that contains the whole market, rather than trying to predict which style will lead next.

# Stock Splits and Buybacks
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/s/stocksplit.asp

In a stock split a company increases its number of shares and lowers the price in proportion. In a 2-for-1 split, one share at 200 dollars becomes two shares at 100 dollars. The total value you own does not change, like cutting a pizza into more slices. Companies split to make shares look more affordable.

A reverse split does the opposite, combining shares to raise the price, and is often done by struggling companies.

A buyback, or share repurchase, is when a company uses cash to buy its own shares. With fewer shares outstanding, each remaining share represents a slightly larger part of the company and earnings per share rise. Buybacks are one way, alongside dividends, that companies return money to shareholders.

# Market Orders and Limit Orders
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/how-stock-markets-work/types-orders

An order is an instruction to buy or sell. A market order executes right away at the best available price. It is certain to trade, but the price is not guaranteed and can differ from the last quote, especially for thinly traded stocks or in fast markets.

A limit order sets the worst price you will accept: a buy limit at 50 dollars only executes at 50 or lower. It controls price but may never be filled.

A stop order becomes a market order once the price reaches a trigger level and is often used to limit losses. Because it then trades at the market, the final price can be worse than the trigger.

Every stock has a bid, the highest price buyers offer, and an ask, the lowest price sellers accept. The gap is the bid-ask spread, a small cost of trading.

# Common Stock and Preferred Stock
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/preferredstock.asp

Most shares that investors buy are common stock. Common shareholders usually have voting rights and benefit fully if the company grows, but they are last in line for dividends and for any money left if the company fails.

Preferred stock sits between stocks and bonds. It usually pays a fixed dividend that must be paid before any dividend to common shareholders, and preferred holders rank ahead of common holders in a bankruptcy. In exchange, preferred shares usually carry no vote and their price does not rise as much when the company does well. Like bonds, their prices are sensitive to interest rates.
