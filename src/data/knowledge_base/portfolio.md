# Diversification
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/diversification

Diversification means spreading money across many different investments so that no single one can do serious damage. It is the idea behind "don't put all your eggs in one basket".

It works because investments do not all move together. When one company, industry or country has a bad year, others may hold up. You can diversify across companies, across sectors such as technology, healthcare and energy, across asset classes such as stocks, bonds and cash, and across countries.

Diversification reduces the risk tied to individual companies, but it cannot remove market risk: in a broad downturn most stocks fall together. Holding 20 technology stocks is not diversified, because they share the same risks. The simplest way to diversify is a broad index fund that holds hundreds or thousands of securities.

# Asset Allocation
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation

Asset allocation is how you divide a portfolio among the main asset classes: stocks, bonds and cash. It is widely regarded as the decision with the biggest influence on both the long-term return and the size of the swings along the way.

Stocks provide growth with high volatility, bonds provide income with moderate volatility, and cash provides stability with little growth. A portfolio of 80 percent stocks and 20 percent bonds will grow more over time, and fall more in a crash, than one with 40 percent stocks and 60 percent bonds.

The right mix depends on your time horizon, your goals and how much risk you can accept. A long horizon can hold more stocks; a short horizon calls for more bonds and cash. There is no single correct allocation.

# Rebalancing
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/r/rebalancing.asp

Rebalancing means bringing a portfolio back to its target mix after market moves have pushed it away. Suppose your target is 60 percent stocks and 40 percent bonds. After a strong year for stocks you might have 70 percent stocks, which is more risk than you chose. Rebalancing sells some stocks and buys bonds, or directs new money to bonds, until the mix is 60/40 again.

Its purpose is to control risk, not to increase return. It also builds in a habit of trimming what has risen and adding to what has fallen.

Common approaches are rebalancing on a schedule, such as once a year, or when an asset class drifts more than a set amount, such as 5 percentage points, from its target. In taxable accounts selling can create a tax bill, so many investors rebalance with new contributions or inside retirement accounts.

# Risk Tolerance and Risk Capacity
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/r/risktolerance.asp

Risk tolerance is your emotional willingness to see your investments fall in value without abandoning your plan. Risk capacity is your financial ability to absorb losses, which depends on your time horizon, income stability, savings and how essential the goal is.

The two can differ. A young investor with a steady job has high capacity but may panic in a downturn. A retiree may feel comfortable with risk yet lack the time to recover from a large loss. A sensible plan respects the lower of the two.

A practical test: if a portfolio fell 30 percent in a year, would you hold, buy more or sell? Investors who sell at the bottom lock in losses, so an allocation you can stay with through a downturn usually beats a more aggressive one you abandon.

# Concentration Risk
source: FINRA
url: https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/concentration-risk

Concentration risk is the danger of having too much of a portfolio in one investment, one industry or one type of asset. If that area performs badly, the whole portfolio suffers.

Concentration often builds up unintentionally: a stock that has risen sharply grows into a large share of the portfolio, an employee accumulates company stock, or several funds turn out to hold the same large companies.

There are no fixed limits, but many guidelines treat a single stock above roughly 10 to 20 percent of a portfolio, or a single sector above roughly a third, as worth a second look. A common way to summarise concentration is the effective number of holdings: a portfolio with ten positions where one is 70 percent behaves more like two holdings than ten. Broad funds are diversified inside, so a large weight in one of them is not the same as a large weight in one company.

# Modern Portfolio Theory
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/m/modernportfoliotheory.asp

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), developed by Harry Markowitz in the 1950s, says an investment should be judged by how it affects the risk and return of the whole portfolio, not on its own.

The key idea is correlation, which measures how closely two investments move together. Combining assets that do not move in step lowers the portfolio's overall volatility without necessarily lowering its expected return. This is the mathematical case for diversification.

For each level of risk there is a mix of assets with the highest expected return. These best mixes form the efficient frontier. A portfolio below the frontier is taking more risk than necessary for its return.

MPT relies on estimates of returns, volatility and correlations that change over time, and correlations often rise in a crisis, so it is a framework for thinking, not a precise forecast.

# Volatility and Beta
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/v/volatility.asp

Volatility describes how much an investment's price moves up and down. It is usually measured by standard deviation, the typical distance of returns from their average. A fund with an average return of 8 percent and a standard deviation of 15 percent will often land anywhere between about -7 and +23 percent in a given year, and sometimes outside that range.

Beta compares a stock's movements with the overall market. A beta of 1 means it tends to move with the market. A beta of 1.3 means it tends to move about 30 percent more, up and down. A beta of 0.7 means it tends to move less.

High volatility is not the same as a permanent loss, but it matters if you may need to sell during a fall. Stocks are more volatile than bonds, and single stocks more than diversified funds.

# The Sharpe Ratio
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/s/sharperatio.asp

The Sharpe ratio measures how much return an investment has produced for each unit of risk taken. It is calculated as the investment's return minus the risk-free rate (such as the Treasury bill yield), divided by the standard deviation of its returns.

Example: a fund returning 9 percent with 12 percent volatility when the risk-free rate is 3 percent has a Sharpe ratio of (9 - 3) / 12 = 0.5. Another fund returning 11 percent with 20 percent volatility scores (11 - 3) / 20 = 0.4. The second earned more but was less efficient at turning risk into return.

Higher is better, and it is mainly used to compare similar funds or portfolios. It is based on past data and assumes that volatility captures all the risk, which is not always true.

# The Three-Fund Portfolio
source: Bogleheads Wiki
url: https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Three-fund_portfolio

The three-fund portfolio is a simple approach popular with index investors. It uses three broad, low-cost index funds: a total domestic stock market fund, a total international stock market fund, and a total bond market fund.

Together these hold thousands of stocks and bonds across the world, so the portfolio is highly diversified, cheap and easy to maintain. The investor's main decisions are how much to hold in stocks versus bonds, and how much of the stock portion to hold internationally.

Example mixes are illustrations, not recommendations: a younger investor might hold a high share of stocks, while someone close to retirement might hold more bonds. The approach reflects the principles of diversification, low cost and staying the course.

# Dollar-Cost Averaging
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/d/dollarcostaveraging.asp

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) means investing a fixed amount at regular intervals, such as 300 dollars every month, whatever the market is doing. Because the amount is fixed, you buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when prices are high, which evens out your average purchase price over time.

Its main benefits are behavioural. It removes the temptation to time the market, builds a saving habit and makes downturns easier to live with, since lower prices mean your next contribution buys more. Regular contributions to a workplace retirement plan are a form of DCA.

If you already hold a lump sum, studies show that investing it all at once has done better than spreading it out about two thirds of the time, because markets rise more often than they fall. Spreading it out reduces the regret if prices drop soon after.

# Correlation
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/correlation.asp

Correlation measures how two investments move in relation to each other, on a scale from -1 to +1. A value of +1 means they move in the same direction all the time, 0 means there is no relationship, and -1 means they move in opposite directions.

Correlation is what makes diversification work. Two risky assets with low correlation can be combined into a portfolio that is less volatile than either alone, because their ups and downs partly cancel. Stocks in the same industry tend to be highly correlated, so they diversify each other poorly. Stocks and high-quality bonds have often had low or negative correlation, which is why they are commonly held together.

Correlations are not fixed. In severe market stress many assets fall at once, so diversification benefits can shrink just when they are most wanted.
