# What Is an ETF?
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-2

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a fund that holds a basket of investments, such as stocks or bonds, and trades on a stock exchange like a single share. Buying one share of an ETF gives you a small slice of everything the fund holds. An S&P 500 ETF, for example, holds about 500 large US companies.

Most ETFs track an index and are passively managed, which keeps costs low. They can be bought and sold at any time during market hours at the current market price.

ETFs are popular with beginners because one purchase provides instant diversification at low cost. They still carry the risk of what they hold: a stock ETF falls when the stock market falls. Narrow ETFs that follow a single sector or theme are much less diversified than broad-market ones.

# Mutual Funds
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-1

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it in stocks, bonds or other assets according to a stated objective. Each investor owns shares of the fund, and each share represents a part of all its holdings.

Mutual funds are bought and sold directly with the fund company, once a day, at the net asset value (NAV) calculated after the market closes. Many have a minimum first investment.

Funds can be actively managed, where a manager picks investments to try to beat the market, or passively managed, where the fund simply follows an index. Costs matter: funds charge an expense ratio, and some charge sales fees called loads. Mutual funds must pass on realised capital gains to shareholders each year, which can create a tax bill in a taxable account.

# Index Funds
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/i/indexfund.asp

An index fund is a mutual fund or ETF built to match the performance of a market index, such as the S&P 500 or a total stock market index, rather than to beat it. It simply holds all, or a representative sample, of the securities in the index.

Because no team is paid to pick stocks and trading is low, index funds have very low costs, often below 0.10 percent a year. They are also broadly diversified and tax-efficient.

Research over many decades has found that most actively managed funds fail to beat their index after fees over long periods. This is the main reason index funds are widely suggested as a core holding for long-term investors. An index fund will never beat the market, and it falls fully when the market falls.

# ETFs Compared With Mutual Funds
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/articles/exchangetradedfunds/08/etf-mutual-fund-difference.asp

ETFs and mutual funds are both pooled, diversified investments, and many track the same indexes. The main differences are practical.

Trading: ETFs trade on an exchange all day at market prices. Mutual funds trade once a day at the closing net asset value. Minimums: an ETF can be bought one share at a time, or less with fractional shares; mutual funds often require a minimum such as 1,000 dollars. Costs: both can be cheap when they track an index, but ETFs involve a small bid-ask spread. Taxes: ETFs are usually more tax-efficient in taxable accounts because of how shares are created and redeemed. Automation: mutual funds make automatic fixed-dollar investing simple.

For a long-term investor holding a broad index, the choice often matters far less than the fund's cost and what it holds.

# Expense Ratios
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/e/expenseratio.asp

The expense ratio is the yearly cost of owning a fund, shown as a percentage of your investment. It covers management and operating costs and is taken out of the fund's returns automatically, so you never see a bill. A 0.05 percent expense ratio costs 5 dollars a year for every 10,000 dollars invested; a 1 percent ratio costs 100 dollars.

Small differences compound into large amounts. On 10,000 dollars growing at 6 percent before costs for 30 years, a 0.05 percent fund ends near 56,600 dollars, while a 1 percent fund ends near 43,200 dollars, about 13,000 dollars less.

Broad index funds and ETFs commonly charge below 0.10 percent. Actively managed funds often charge 0.5 to 1 percent or more. Cost is one of the few things about an investment that you can know and control in advance.

# Target-Date Funds
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/t/target-date_fund.asp

A target-date fund is an all-in-one fund designed around the year you expect to need the money, such as "Target 2055" for someone retiring around 2055. It holds a mix of stock and bond funds and automatically shifts that mix from mostly stocks toward more bonds and cash as the target year approaches. This planned shift is called the glide path.

The appeal is simplicity: one fund handles diversification, asset allocation and rebalancing. They are a common default in workplace retirement plans.

Points to check: funds with the same target year can hold quite different stock percentages, and costs vary widely, so the expense ratio is worth comparing. A target-date fund does not guarantee enough money at the target date.

# Active and Passive Investing
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/passiveinvesting.asp

Active investing tries to beat the market by choosing which investments to buy and when. It needs research and frequent trading, so it costs more. Passive investing tries only to match the market by holding an index fund for the long term, which keeps costs and trading low.

The case for passive investing rests on arithmetic: before costs, the average actively managed dollar earns the market return, so after higher costs the average active fund must trail it. Long-term scorecards consistently show that a large majority of active funds underperform their index over 10 years or more, and that past winners rarely keep winning.

Active management can still play a role in some areas, and some investors enjoy it. Many use a low-cost passive core for most of their money.

# Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/real-estate-investment-trusts-reits

A real estate investment trust (REIT) is a company that owns, and usually operates, income-producing property such as apartments, offices, warehouses, shopping centres or data centres. Most REITs trade on stock exchanges, so investors can own a share of large property portfolios without buying buildings.

By law REITs must pay out most of their taxable income to shareholders, so they typically offer higher dividend yields than ordinary stocks. Those dividends are mostly taxed as ordinary income.

REITs add a different source of return to a portfolio, but they are still stock-like in risk. Their prices are sensitive to interest rates and to the property market. REIT index funds spread the risk across many companies and property types.

# Net Asset Value (NAV)
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/n/nav.asp

Net asset value is the per-share value of a fund: the total value of everything the fund owns, minus what it owes, divided by the number of shares. If a fund holds 100 million dollars of assets, has no liabilities and has 4 million shares, its NAV is 25 dollars.

Mutual funds calculate NAV once each trading day after the market closes, and all buy and sell orders that day are filled at that price. ETFs also have a NAV, but they trade during the day at a market price that can be slightly above it (a premium) or below it (a discount). For large, liquid ETFs the difference is normally tiny.

A fund's NAV by itself says nothing about whether it is cheap or expensive; a 10 dollar fund is not a better deal than a 100 dollar one.
