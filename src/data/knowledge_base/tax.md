# Capital Gains Tax
source: IRS
url: https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409

A capital gain is the profit from selling an investment for more than you paid. The amount you paid, adjusted for certain items, is called the cost basis. Tax is generally due only when you sell, which is called realising the gain. A rise in value that you have not sold is an unrealised gain and is not taxed.

In the United States the holding period matters. Gains on assets held one year or less are short-term and taxed at ordinary income rates. Gains on assets held longer than one year are long-term and taxed at lower rates of 0, 15 or 20 percent depending on income.

Example: buying shares for 5,000 dollars and selling for 7,000 dollars creates a 2,000 dollar gain. Losses can offset gains. Rates and thresholds change every year, so check current official figures.

# Tax-Advantaged Accounts
source: IRS
url: https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans

Tax-advantaged accounts give tax benefits to encourage saving for retirement, health or education. They come in two main types.

Tax-deferred accounts, such as a traditional 401(k) or traditional IRA, usually let you contribute pre-tax money. The investments grow without yearly tax, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.

Tax-free accounts, such as a Roth IRA or Roth 401(k), take after-tax contributions. Growth and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

A regular brokerage account, by contrast, is taxable: dividends, interest and realised gains are taxed each year. Tax-advantaged accounts have yearly contribution limits and rules on when money can come out, with penalties for most early withdrawals. Not paying tax on growth every year lets more money compound.

# Traditional IRA and Roth IRA
source: IRS
url: https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/traditional-and-roth-iras

An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is a US retirement account you open yourself. The two main kinds differ in when you pay tax.

With a traditional IRA, contributions may be tax-deductible now, investments grow tax-deferred, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as income. Required minimum distributions begin in your seventies. With a Roth IRA, contributions are made with after-tax money and are not deductible, but growth and qualified withdrawals after age 59 and a half are tax-free, and there are no required distributions for the original owner. Roth contributions are limited above certain income levels.

A general way to compare them: a traditional IRA tends to help more if your tax rate will be lower in retirement, a Roth if it will be higher. The yearly contribution limit is several thousand dollars and changes, so check the current IRS figure.

# 401(k) Plans and the Employer Match
source: IRS
url: https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/401k-plans

A 401(k) is a retirement plan offered by US employers. You choose a percentage of each paycheck to contribute, and it is invested in funds from the plan's menu. Traditional contributions come out before income tax and are taxed when withdrawn; many plans also offer a Roth option with after-tax contributions and tax-free qualified withdrawals.

Many employers add a match. A common formula is 50 cents for every dollar you contribute, up to 6 percent of salary. On a 60,000 dollar salary, contributing 6 percent (3,600 dollars) would bring 1,800 dollars from the employer, an immediate 50 percent return that is lost if you contribute less.

Matching money may vest over several years. Withdrawals before age 59 and a half usually incur income tax plus a 10 percent penalty. Yearly limits are much higher than for IRAs and change each year.

# Tax-Loss Harvesting and the Wash-Sale Rule
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/t/taxgainlossharvesting.asp

Tax-loss harvesting means selling an investment that has fallen below its purchase price in order to realise the loss, then using that loss to reduce tax. Realised losses first offset realised capital gains. In the United States, if losses exceed gains, up to 3,000 dollars a year can offset ordinary income, and the remainder carries forward to future years.

Investors usually buy a similar but not identical investment so that they stay in the market.

The wash-sale rule limits this. If you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss cannot be claimed now and is added to the cost basis of the new purchase instead. Harvesting applies only in taxable accounts, and it defers tax rather than removing it, because the replacement has a lower cost basis.

# How Dividends and Interest Are Taxed
source: IRS
url: https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc404

In a US taxable account, investment income is taxed in the year it is received, even if you reinvest it.

Dividends are either qualified or ordinary. Qualified dividends, which come from most US companies and many foreign ones when holding-period rules are met, are taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates. Ordinary, or non-qualified, dividends are taxed at your regular income tax rate; most REIT dividends fall in this group.

Interest from bank accounts, CDs and corporate bonds is taxed as ordinary income. Interest on Treasury securities is exempt from state and local income tax. Interest on most municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax.

Inside tax-advantaged accounts such as IRAs and 401(k)s, dividends and interest are not taxed as they are received.

# Health Savings Accounts
source: IRS
url: https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969

A Health Savings Account (HSA) is a US account for medical costs, available to people covered by a qualifying high-deductible health plan. It is often described as triple tax-advantaged: contributions are tax-deductible or pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free.

Unlike a flexible spending account, the balance rolls over from year to year and stays with you if you change jobs. Many HSAs allow the balance to be invested once it passes a minimum.

After age 65, money can be withdrawn for any purpose without penalty, although non-medical withdrawals are taxed as income, which makes the account work like a traditional IRA at that point. Before 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed and penalised. Contribution limits change each year.

# Tax-Efficient Investing and Asset Location
source: Bogleheads Wiki
url: https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Tax-efficient_fund_placement

Asset location means choosing which type of account holds which investment, in order to reduce the total tax paid. It is separate from asset allocation, which is how much you hold of each asset.

The general principle is to hold tax-inefficient investments, which produce a lot of taxable income, inside tax-advantaged accounts, and tax-efficient investments in taxable accounts. Bond funds, REITs and actively managed funds that trade often tend to be tax-inefficient. Broad stock index funds and ETFs are tax-efficient because they trade little and pay mostly qualified dividends.

Other habits that reduce tax drag include holding investments for more than a year to qualify for long-term rates, using new contributions to rebalance instead of selling, and keeping turnover low. Tax should guide where things are held, not override a sound overall allocation.

# Cost Basis
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/costbasis.asp

Cost basis is the original value of an investment for tax purposes, usually the purchase price plus commissions and fees. When you sell, the capital gain or loss is the sale proceeds minus the cost basis.

Reinvested dividends raise your cost basis because each reinvestment is a new purchase. Forgetting this can lead to paying tax twice on the same money.

If you bought shares at different times and prices, the method used to decide which shares were sold affects the tax. First in, first out (FIFO) sells the oldest shares first. Specific identification lets you choose particular lots, for example the ones with the highest cost, to reduce the gain. Mutual funds may also use average cost. Brokers in the United States report cost basis for most securities bought in recent years.

# Tax-Advantaged Accounts Outside the United States
source: GOV.UK and India Income Tax Department
url: https://www.gov.uk/individual-savings-accounts

Tax rules are different in every country. A few common accounts:

United Kingdom: an Individual Savings Account (ISA) lets residents save or invest up to a yearly allowance with no tax on interest, dividends or gains. A Stocks and Shares ISA holds investments; a Cash ISA holds savings.

India: the Public Provident Fund (PPF) is a government-backed long-term savings scheme with a 15-year term and tax benefits. An Equity Linked Savings Scheme (ELSS) is an equity mutual fund with a three-year lock-in that has qualified for a deduction under Section 80C in the old tax regime. The National Pension System (NPS) is a retirement scheme with its own deductions.

Canada offers the TFSA and RRSP, which resemble Roth and traditional accounts. Limits and rules change often, so always confirm with the official tax authority of your country.

# Required Minimum Distributions and Early Withdrawals
source: IRS
url: https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plan-and-ira-required-minimum-distributions-faqs

US retirement accounts have rules about when money must and must not come out.

Early withdrawals: taking money from a traditional IRA or 401(k) before age 59 and a half generally triggers ordinary income tax plus a 10 percent penalty, with exceptions for certain situations such as disability or specific first-home and education costs for IRAs. Roth IRA contributions, but not earnings, can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty.

Required minimum distributions (RMDs): because traditional accounts have never been taxed, the government requires withdrawals to begin in your seventies. The amount is based on the account balance and life expectancy, and missing one leads to a penalty. Roth IRAs have no RMDs for the original owner.

The exact ages and penalties have changed several times, so check the current IRS rules.
