# What Is a Bond?
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds-or-fixed-income-products/bonds

A bond is a loan from an investor to a government or company. The issuer promises to pay interest, called the coupon, at set times and to repay the original amount, called the principal or face value, on the maturity date.

Example: a 1,000 dollar bond with a 4 percent coupon and a 10-year maturity pays 40 dollars a year for 10 years and then returns the 1,000 dollars.

Bonds are generally less risky than stocks and are used for income and stability. Their main risks are credit risk, the chance the issuer cannot pay; interest rate risk, the fall in a bond's price when rates rise; and inflation risk, because fixed payments buy less over time.

# Bond Prices and Interest Rates
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/interest-rate-risk

Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. When market interest rates rise, existing bonds with lower coupons become less attractive, so their prices fall. When rates fall, existing bonds with higher coupons become more valuable and their prices rise.

Example: you hold a bond paying 3 percent. If new bonds start paying 5 percent, nobody will pay full price for yours, so its price drops until its return matches the market.

The longer a bond's maturity, the more its price moves for a given rate change. If you hold an individual bond to maturity and the issuer does not default, you still receive the full face value; the price swings along the way matter mainly if you sell early or hold a bond fund.

# Bond Yield and Yield to Maturity
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/y/yieldtomaturity.asp

Yield is the return a bond provides. The coupon rate is fixed when the bond is issued. Current yield is the yearly coupon divided by today's price: a bond paying 40 dollars a year that now trades at 800 dollars has a current yield of 5 percent.

Yield to maturity (YTM) is the total yearly return you would earn if you bought the bond today, held it until it matures and received every payment. It includes both the coupons and the gain or loss between today's price and the face value. A bond bought below face value has a YTM above its coupon rate; one bought above face value has a YTM below it.

YTM lets you compare bonds with different prices, coupons and maturities on one scale.

# Credit Ratings
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/creditrating.asp

A credit rating is an independent opinion of how likely a bond issuer is to pay its debts on time. The main rating agencies are S&P Global, Moody's and Fitch. Ratings run from AAA, the strongest, down to D, which means default.

Bonds rated BBB- or higher (Baa3 at Moody's) are called investment grade. Bonds rated below that are called high-yield or junk bonds. They pay higher interest to make up for a higher chance of default, and their prices tend to fall when the economy weakens, behaving more like stocks.

Ratings can change, and a downgrade usually pushes a bond's price down. A rating is an opinion, not a guarantee, so diversifying across many issuers still matters.

# Treasury Securities
source: TreasuryDirect
url: https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/

Treasury securities are debt issued by the United States government and are considered to have very low credit risk because they are backed by the government. They come in several types. Treasury bills mature in one year or less and are sold at a discount instead of paying a coupon. Treasury notes mature in 2 to 10 years and Treasury bonds in 20 or 30 years; both pay interest every six months.

Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) adjust their principal with inflation, so they protect purchasing power. Series I savings bonds also pay an inflation-linked rate.

Interest on Treasuries is subject to federal income tax but exempt from state and local income tax. Their prices still move with interest rates, so longer-dated Treasuries can fall in value when rates rise.

# Duration
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/d/duration.asp

Duration measures how sensitive a bond or bond fund is to changes in interest rates. It is stated in years, and a rule of thumb is that for each 1 percentage point rise in rates, the price falls by about the duration in percent. A fund with a duration of 6 years would lose roughly 6 percent if rates rose 1 point, and gain about 6 percent if rates fell 1 point.

Longer maturities and lower coupons mean higher duration. Short-term bond funds have low duration, around 1 to 3 years, and are more stable. Long-term bond funds can have durations above 15 years and swing much more.

Matching duration to your time horizon is a common way to manage interest rate risk: money needed soon is usually kept in short-duration holdings.

# Bond Funds and Individual Bonds
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/b/bondfund.asp

You can own bonds directly or through a bond fund or ETF. An individual bond has a fixed maturity: if the issuer does not default you get the face value back on that date, whatever happened to prices in between. Buying a diversified set of individual bonds, however, takes a lot of money and effort.

A bond fund holds hundreds or thousands of bonds, so it is diversified and easy to buy in small amounts. It has no maturity date. The fund keeps replacing bonds as they mature, so its price keeps moving with interest rates and there is no guaranteed amount at a future date.

Bond funds charge an expense ratio. For most beginners a low-cost broad bond fund is the simplest way to get bond exposure.

# Certificates of Deposit and Money Market Funds
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/certificateofdeposit.asp

A certificate of deposit (CD) is a bank deposit that pays a fixed interest rate for a fixed term, such as six months or five years. In return for a higher rate than a savings account you agree to leave the money in place; withdrawing early usually costs a penalty. In the United States CDs at insured banks are covered by FDIC insurance up to the legal limit.

A money market fund is a mutual fund that holds very short-term, high-quality debt and aims to keep a stable value of one dollar per share. It is easy to get money in and out, and its yield follows short-term interest rates. It is not a bank deposit and is not FDIC insured.

Both are used for short-term goals and emergency funds, where stability matters more than growth.

# Municipal and Corporate Bonds
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds-or-fixed-income-products/municipal-bonds

Corporate bonds are issued by companies. They pay more interest than government bonds of the same maturity because companies are more likely to default. The extra yield is called the credit spread, and it widens when investors worry about the economy.

Municipal bonds, or munis, are issued by states, cities and other local governments in the United States to pay for things like schools and roads. Their interest is generally exempt from federal income tax, and often from state tax for residents of the issuing state. Because of this their stated yields are lower, and they tend to suit investors in higher tax brackets holding them in taxable accounts.

Both types carry credit risk and interest rate risk, and both are available through diversified bond funds.
