# Stock Market Indexes
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/m/marketindex.asp

A market index tracks the performance of a group of securities and serves as a yardstick for a market or a part of it. When people say "the market was up today" they usually mean an index.

The S&P 500 follows about 500 large US companies and is weighted by market capitalisation, so the largest companies move it most. The Dow Jones Industrial Average follows 30 large US companies and is weighted by share price. The Nasdaq Composite covers the stocks listed on the Nasdaq exchange and leans toward technology. The Russell 2000 follows small US companies, and indexes such as MSCI EAFE follow developed markets outside North America.

You cannot buy an index directly, but index funds and ETFs are built to match one. Indexes are also the benchmark against which fund managers are judged.

# Bull and Bear Markets
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/b/bullmarket.asp

A bull market is a long period of rising prices, commonly defined as a rise of 20 percent or more from a recent low. A bear market is a fall of 20 percent or more from a recent high. A correction is a smaller fall of 10 to 20 percent.

Bear markets are a normal part of investing. Since the Second World War the US stock market has had one roughly every five to six years on average. They have typically lasted around a year, while bull markets have lasted several years and gained far more than bear markets lost.

Nobody can reliably predict when either will start or end. Investors who sold during bear markets often missed the recovery, since some of the market's best days come shortly after its worst.

# Interest Rates and the Federal Reserve
source: Federal Reserve
url: https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm

The Federal Reserve, or the Fed, is the central bank of the United States. Its main tool is the federal funds rate, the rate at which banks lend to each other overnight, which influences borrowing costs across the whole economy.

When inflation is too high the Fed raises rates. Borrowing becomes more expensive, spending and investment slow, and price rises cool. When the economy is weak it lowers rates to encourage borrowing and spending.

Rate changes affect investments. Rising rates push existing bond prices down and raise the yield on savings and new bonds. They also tend to weigh on stock prices, especially fast-growing companies, because future profits are worth less today and borrowing costs more. Falling rates usually do the opposite. Markets often move on expectations before the Fed acts.

# Recessions and the Business Cycle
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/r/recession.asp

Economies move in cycles of expansion and contraction. In an expansion, output, employment and profits grow. At a peak, growth slows. A recession is a significant, widespread decline in economic activity lasting more than a few months, commonly described as two consecutive quarters of falling GDP. A trough marks the bottom before recovery begins.

In recessions unemployment rises, companies earn less and stock prices often fall, although the stock market usually starts falling before a recession is announced and starts recovering before it ends. This makes it a leading indicator.

Recessions are a regular feature of economies and have always been followed by recovery. For long-term investors they are a reason to keep an emergency fund and a suitable asset allocation, more than a reason to leave the market.

# Market Volatility and Corrections
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/volatility

Market volatility is the size and speed of price changes. Calm markets move a fraction of a percent a day; in turbulent periods daily moves of 2 or 3 percent are common. The VIX index, sometimes called the fear gauge, measures the volatility investors expect in the S&P 500 over the next 30 days.

Falls are frequent. A drop of 10 percent or more within a year has happened in around half of all years, and the market has still finished most of those years higher.

Volatility feels worst when you watch daily, but it matters most when you must sell. Ways investors live with it include holding an allocation that matches their timeline, keeping money needed soon out of stocks, diversifying, and continuing regular contributions so that lower prices buy more shares.

# How to Read a Stock Quote
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/04/021204.asp

A stock quote shows the latest trading information for a security. The ticker symbol is its short code, such as AAPL for Apple. The price is the last traded price. The change and percentage change compare that price with the previous close, yesterday's final price.

Open, high and low give the first price of the day and the day's range. The 52-week high and low show the range over the past year, which gives context: a price near the 52-week high has risen strongly, though that alone does not say whether it is expensive. Volume is the number of shares traded and indicates liquidity. Bid and ask are the best current prices to sell and buy.

Many quotes also show market cap, P/E ratio and dividend yield. Free quotes are often delayed by about 15 minutes.

# Economic Indicators
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/e/economic_indicator.asp

Economic indicators are statistics that describe the health of the economy. Investors watch them because they influence company profits and interest rate decisions.

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total value of goods and services produced and is the broadest measure of growth. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures inflation. The unemployment rate and monthly jobs report show the strength of the labour market. Consumer confidence and retail sales show how willing households are to spend, and purchasing managers' indexes show business activity.

Markets react to how a number compares with what was expected, not to the number itself. Strong jobs data can even push stocks down if it makes interest rate rises more likely. For long-term investors single reports matter little; the trend over months matters more.

# Market Timing
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/m/markettiming.asp

Market timing is trying to move in and out of investments to avoid falls and catch rises. It is appealing and very hard to do, because it needs two correct decisions: when to sell and when to buy back.

The market's largest gains tend to be packed into a small number of days, often close to its worst days. Studies of the S&P 500 over 20-year periods have found that missing just the 10 best days cut the final return roughly in half. Investors who sell after a fall are likely to be out of the market when it rebounds.

For this reason long-term investors are usually encouraged to choose an allocation they can hold through downturns and keep investing regularly. The saying is "time in the market, not timing the market".

# Behavioral Biases in Investing
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/b/behavioralfinance.asp

Behavioural finance studies how psychology leads investors to make predictable mistakes. Common biases include the following.

Loss aversion: losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains please, which leads to panic selling or holding losers too long. Herding: following the crowd into what is popular, often near the top. Recency bias: assuming recent trends will continue. Overconfidence: overrating one's own skill, which leads to too much trading and too little diversification. Confirmation bias: looking only for information that supports an existing view. Anchoring: fixing on an irrelevant number such as the price you paid.

Defences are mostly procedural: a written plan, automatic contributions, a fixed rebalancing rule, diversification and checking the portfolio less often.

# How News Affects Markets
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/155.asp

Prices move when new information changes what investors expect. Company news such as earnings, forecasts, product launches, lawsuits or mergers mainly moves that company's stock. Economic news such as inflation data, jobs reports and central bank decisions moves the whole market. Political and world events can move markets through their effect on trade, energy prices or confidence.

What matters is the surprise. Markets look ahead, so widely expected news is already reflected in prices; this is why a stock can fall on good results that were slightly below expectations.

Headlines are written to attract attention and often exaggerate the importance of a single day. A headline alone rarely says enough to judge the long-term effect. For long-term investors most daily news is noise, and acting on it tends to increase trading costs and mistakes.

# Cryptocurrency Basics
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/spotlight/crypto-assets

Cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin and Ether are digital assets recorded on a blockchain, a shared ledger maintained by a network of computers instead of a bank or government. They can be traded on crypto exchanges and, in some countries, through exchange-traded funds.

They differ from stocks and bonds in important ways. They produce no earnings or interest, so their price depends entirely on what buyers will pay. Prices are extremely volatile; falls of more than 50 percent have happened several times. Regulation and investor protection are weaker than for traditional securities, and fraud, hacks and exchange failures have caused real losses.

Regulators describe crypto assets as highly speculative. People who choose to hold them commonly keep the amount small enough that a total loss would not affect their goals.

# Exchanges and How Trading Works
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/how-stock-markets-work

A stock exchange is a marketplace where buyers and sellers of securities are matched. In the United States the largest are the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq. Regular trading hours are 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern time on weekdays, with limited trading before and after.

Individual investors trade through a broker, which sends their orders to an exchange or market maker. Market makers stand ready to buy and sell, which keeps trading smooth; they earn the bid-ask spread.

After a trade, settlement, the official exchange of money and shares, takes one business day in the US. Brokerage accounts are covered by SIPC protection up to set limits if the broker fails; this covers missing assets, not investment losses. Regulators such as the SEC oversee the markets.
