# What Is Investing?
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing

Investing means putting money to work in assets such as stocks, bonds or funds with the expectation that they will grow in value or pay income over time. It is different from saving: savings held in a bank account are stable and easy to reach, but they usually grow slowly. Investments can grow faster, yet their value moves up and down, and you can lose money.

People invest to reach long-term goals such as retirement, a home or education. The earlier you start, the more time your money has to grow. A simple example: 200 dollars invested every month for 30 years is 72,000 dollars of contributions, but at an assumed 6 percent yearly return it could grow to roughly 200,000 dollars. The return is an assumption, not a promise.

# Compound Interest
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/compoundinterest.asp

Compound interest is interest earned on both your original money and on the interest it has already earned. Simple interest pays only on the original amount, so growth is a straight line. Compounding makes growth curve upward, slowly at first and faster later.

Example: 1,000 dollars at 5 percent simple interest earns 50 dollars every year, reaching 1,500 dollars after 10 years. With yearly compounding it reaches about 1,629 dollars, because each year's interest also earns interest. Over 30 years the gap is much larger: 2,500 dollars versus about 4,322 dollars.

Three things drive compounding: the rate of return, how often it compounds, and above all time. This is why starting early matters more than starting with a large amount. Compounding also works against you on debt, such as unpaid credit card balances.

# Risk and Return
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/what-risk

Risk is the chance that an investment's actual result differs from what you expected, including the chance of losing money. Return is what you earn: price gains plus income such as dividends or interest. The two are linked. Investments with higher potential returns generally carry higher risk, and safer investments generally offer lower returns.

Cash and short-term government bills are the most stable but grow the least. Bonds sit in the middle. Stocks have historically delivered the highest long-term returns, but with large swings; broad stock markets have fallen 30 percent or more in some years.

No investment is risk-free. Even cash carries inflation risk, the risk that prices rise faster than your money grows. The useful question is not "how do I avoid risk?" but "how much risk fits my goal and timeline?"

# Inflation and Purchasing Power
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/i/inflation.asp

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. As prices rise, each dollar buys less; this loss is called a fall in purchasing power. At 3 percent inflation, something that costs 100 dollars today costs about 134 dollars in 10 years and about 181 dollars in 20 years.

Inflation matters for investors in two ways. First, a goal set in today's money needs a larger amount in the future, so a plan should grow the target with inflation. Second, what counts is the real return, which is the return after inflation. An account earning 2 percent while inflation is 3 percent is losing about 1 percent of purchasing power a year.

In the United States inflation is most often measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI). Assets such as stocks and inflation-protected bonds have historically helped investors keep up with inflation over long periods.

# Time Horizon
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/assessing-your-risk-tolerance

Your time horizon is how long you expect to keep money invested before you need it. It is one of the most important inputs to any plan, because it decides how much short-term risk you can afford to take.

Money needed within about three years, such as a house deposit, has a short horizon. A market fall just before you need it could leave you short, so stable holdings such as savings accounts or short-term bonds are usually considered. Money needed in more than ten years, such as retirement savings for a 30-year-old, has a long horizon. It has time to recover from downturns, so it can hold more stocks.

Many investors shift gradually from stocks toward bonds and cash as a goal approaches. Target-date funds do this automatically.

# Saving Versus Investing
source: SEC Investor.gov
url: https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/save-and-invest

Saving and investing do different jobs. Saving means keeping money in safe, easy-to-reach places such as an insured bank account. The balance does not fall, but the interest is usually low. Investing means buying assets such as stocks and bonds that can grow more, in exchange for the risk that their value drops.

A common order of steps is: build an emergency fund in savings, pay down high-interest debt, then invest for long-term goals. Use savings for money you need soon or cannot afford to lose. Use investments for goals that are years away.

Example: an emergency fund and next year's holiday belong in savings. Retirement in 25 years is a job for investments, because over that time inflation would wear down cash.

# Emergency Fund
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/e/emergency_fund.asp

An emergency fund is money set aside for unexpected costs such as a job loss, a medical bill or a car repair. A common guideline is three to six months of essential living expenses, kept somewhere safe and quick to reach such as a high-yield savings account or a money market account.

The fund exists so that a surprise does not force you to sell investments at a bad time or borrow at high interest. For that reason it is normally kept out of the stock market: its job is to be there, not to grow.

Example: if essential spending is 2,500 dollars a month, a three-to-six month fund is 7,500 to 15,000 dollars. People with unstable income or dependants often aim for the higher end. Many start with a smaller first target, such as 1,000 dollars, and build from there.

# The Rule of 72
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/r/ruleof72.asp

The Rule of 72 is a quick way to estimate how long money takes to double at a fixed yearly return. Divide 72 by the yearly rate in percent. At 6 percent, money doubles in about 72 / 6 = 12 years. At 9 percent it takes about 8 years, and at 3 percent about 24 years.

The rule also works in reverse and for inflation. To double money in 10 years you need roughly 7.2 percent a year. At 3 percent inflation, prices double, and purchasing power halves, in about 24 years.

It is an approximation that works best for rates between about 4 and 12 percent and assumes compounding once a year. It is useful for comparing options in your head, not for precise planning.

# Liquidity
source: Investopedia
url: https://www.investopedia.com/terms/l/liquidity.asp

Liquidity describes how quickly and cheaply an asset can be turned into cash without moving its price much. Cash is the most liquid asset. Shares of large companies and popular ETFs are highly liquid because they trade in large volumes every day. Property, private businesses and collectibles are illiquid: selling can take months and costs can be high.

Liquidity matters because needing cash quickly from an illiquid asset can force you to accept a poor price. It is one reason to keep an emergency fund in liquid form.

For a traded security, signs of good liquidity are high daily trading volume and a narrow bid-ask spread, the small gap between the best buying and selling prices.
